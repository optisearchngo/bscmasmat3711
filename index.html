Solutions: Questions 1–4

MAT3711 · metric spaces, uniform convergence and integration (UNISA 2026 paper)

Question 1

Assume $B\neq\emptyset$ (otherwise $d(\cdot,B)=+\infty$). Note that $d(A,B)=\inf_{a\in A}d(\{a\},B)$.

(a)

Let $x,y\in X$ and $b\in B$. By the triangle inequality $d(x,b)\le d(x,y)+d(y,b)$. Taking the infimum over $b\in B$ gives $g(x)\le d(x,y)+g(y)$. Swapping $x$ and $y$ gives $g(y)\le d(x,y)+g(x)$. Hence
$$|g(x)-g(y)|\le d(x,y)\quad\forall x,y\in X,$$
so $g$ is Lipschitz with $k=1$.
Note: the strict "$\lt$" in the quoted definition cannot hold for $k=1$ in general: in $\mathbb R$ with $B=\{0\}$, $|g(x)-g(y)|=|x-y|$ for $x,y\gt0$. The standard definition uses "$\le$", which is what is proved here.

(b)

Let $A$ be compact and nonempty, $B$ closed, and $A\cap B=\emptyset$.
  • By (a), $g$ is continuous, so on the compact set $A$ it attains its minimum at some $a_0\in A$. Then $d(A,B)=\inf_{a\in A}g(a)=g(a_0)$.
  • Suppose $g(a_0)=0$. For each $n$ there is $b_n\in B$ with $d(a_0,b_n)\lt 1/n$, so $b_n\to a_0$. As $B$ is closed, $a_0\in B$, contradicting $A\cap B=\emptyset$.
  • Hence $d(A,B)=g(a_0)\gt0$. $\blacksquare$
Counterexample (both closed, neither compact). In $\mathbb R^2$ take
$$A=\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb R\},\qquad B=\{(x,1/x):x\gt0\}.$$
Both are closed and $A\cap B=\emptyset$, but $d\big((x,0),(x,1/x)\big)=1/x\to 0$, so $d(A,B)=0$.
If $A\cap B\neq\emptyset$. Pick $x\in A\cap B$; then $d(x,x)=0$, so $d(A,B)=0$ always. Thus for compact $A$ and closed $B$: $d(A,B)\gt0\iff A\cap B=\emptyset$.

(c)

Let $p\neq q$ in $X$ and $r:=d(p,q)\gt0$. Define $f:X\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=d(x,p)$. By (a) with $B=\{p\}$, $f$ is continuous.
$X$ is connected, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem $f[X]$ is an interval. It contains $f(p)=0$ and $f(q)=r$, hence $[0,r]\subseteq f[X]$.
For each $t\in[0,r]$ choose $x_t\in X$ with $f(x_t)=t$. The map $t\mapsto x_t$ is injective because $f(x_t)=t$ differs for different $t$. So $X$ contains an uncountable subset, and $X$ is uncountable. $\blacksquare$

Question 2

(a)(i) Pointwise limit

$f_n(0)=0$ for all $n$, so $f(0)=0$. For $x\in(0,1]$,
$$0\le f_n(x)=\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\le\frac{nx}{n^2x^2}=\frac1{nx}\to0.$$
Answer: $f\equiv 0$ on $[0,1]$.

(a)(ii) Uniform convergence?

No. At $x=1/n\in[0,1]$, $f_n(1/n)=\dfrac{1}{1+1}=\tfrac12$. So $\sup_{[0,1]}|f_n-f|\ge\tfrac12\not\to0$. The convergence is not uniform, even though the limit function is continuous.

(b)(i)

For $a,c\ge0$, $ac\le\frac12(a^2+c^2)$. With $a=\sqrt{a_n}$ and $c=1/n$,
$$0\lt\frac{\sqrt{a_n}}{n}\le\frac12\Big(a_n+\frac1{n^2}\Big).$$
Both $\sum a_n$ and $\sum 1/n^2$ converge, so by the comparison test $\sum\sqrt{a_n}/n$ converges. Its terms are positive, so it converges absolutely. $\blacksquare$

(b)(ii)

The converse is false. Take $a_n=1/n$. Then $\sum a_n$ is the harmonic series, which diverges. But
$$\sum\frac{\sqrt{a_n}}{n}=\sum\frac1{n^{3/2}}$$
converges (p-series with $p=3/2\gt1$).

(c)

Let $\varepsilon\gt0$ and $L=\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)$.
  1. Choose $M\gt0$ with $|f(x)-L|\lt\varepsilon/2$ for all $x\ge M$. Then $|f(x)-f(y)|\lt\varepsilon$ for all $x,y\ge M$.
  2. $[0,M+1]$ is compact and $f$ is continuous, so $f$ is uniformly continuous on it. There is $\delta_1\gt0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|\lt\varepsilon$ whenever $x,y\in[0,M+1]$ and $|x-y|\lt\delta_1$.
  3. Put $\delta=\min(\delta_1,1)$ and let $x\lt y$ with $y-x\lt\delta$.
    • If $y\le M+1$, both points lie in $[0,M+1]$, so step 2 applies.
    • If $y\gt M+1$, then $x\gt y-1\gt M$, so both points are $\ge M$ and step 1 applies.
In either case $|f(x)-f(y)|\lt\varepsilon$, so $f$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb R_{\ge0}$. $\blacksquare$

Question 3

(a)

Let $h_n:=|f-f_n|$. Each $h_n$ is continuous and $h_n(x)\to0$ pointwise. Because $(f_n)$ is monotone and converges to $f$, for each $x$ either $f_n(x)\uparrow f(x)$ (so $h_n=f-f_n\ge0$) or $f_n(x)\downarrow f(x)$ (so $h_n=f_n-f\ge0$). In both cases $h_{n+1}\le h_n$.
Fix $\varepsilon\gt0$ and set $U_n:=\{x\in X:h_n(x)\lt\varepsilon\}$.
  • $U_n$ is open, as the preimage of $(-\infty,\varepsilon)$ under a continuous map.
  • $U_n\subseteq U_{n+1}$, because $h_n$ is non-increasing in $n$.
  • $\bigcup_nU_n=X$, because $h_n(x)\to0$ for every $x$.
By compactness, finitely many $U_{n_1},\dots,U_{n_k}$ cover $X$. With $N=\max n_i$ we get $U_N=X$. For $n\ge N$, $X=U_N\subseteq U_n$, i.e. $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\lt\varepsilon$ for all $x\in X$. Hence $f_n\to f$ uniformly. $\blacksquare$

(b) Both hypotheses are needed

Continuity of $f$ is needed. On $X=[0,1]$ let $f_n(x)=x^n$. These are continuous and decreasing in $n$, with limit $f=0$ on $[0,1)$ and $f(1)=1$. The sequence is monotone but $f$ is discontinuous, and the convergence is not uniform (a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous).
Monotonicity is needed. On $[0,1]$ let $f_n(x)=\dfrac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ (Question 2(a)). These are continuous and the limit $0$ is continuous, but the convergence is not uniform since $\sup|f_n|\ge\frac12$. The sequence is not monotone: at $x=\frac12$ the values for $n=1,2,3$ are $0.4,\ 0.5,\ 0.46\ldots$

Question 4

(a)

Take $g(x)=x(1-x)f(x)$. It is continuous with $g(0)=g(1)=0$, so
$$0=\int_0^1 fg\,dx=\int_0^1x(1-x)f(x)^2\,dx.$$
The integrand $h(x)=x(1-x)f(x)^2$ is continuous and $\ge0$. If $h(x_0)\gt0$ for some $x_0$, then $h\gt h(x_0)/2$ on some interval $[c,d]$ with $c\lt d$, giving $\int_0^1h\ge(d-c)h(x_0)/2\gt0$, a contradiction.
So $x(1-x)f(x)^2=0$ everywhere, hence $f=0$ on $(0,1)$, and by continuity $f(0)=f(1)=0$. Therefore $f=\mathbf 0$. $\blacksquare$

(b)

Since $\int_0^1nx^n\,dx=\frac n{n+1}$,
$$n\int_0^1x^nf(x)\,dx=f(1)\frac n{n+1}+R_n,\qquad R_n:=n\int_0^1x^n\big(f(x)-f(1)\big)dx.$$
The first term tends to $f(1)$; it remains to show $R_n\to0$.
Let $M=\sup_{[0,1]}|f|$ and $\varepsilon\gt0$. By continuity at $1$ choose $\delta\in(0,1)$ with $|f(x)-f(1)|\lt\varepsilon$ on $[1-\delta,1]$. Then
$$|R_n|\le 2M\,n\int_0^{1-\delta}x^n\,dx+\varepsilon\,n\int_{1-\delta}^1x^n\,dx\le 2M(1-\delta)^{n+1}+\varepsilon.$$
Since $(1-\delta)^{n+1}\to0$, $\limsup|R_n|\le\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon\gt0$, so $R_n\to0$ and the limit is $f(1)$. $\blacksquare$

(c)

For $0\lt a\lt b$, the partial derivative $\dfrac{2x}{x^2+\sin^2\theta}$ is continuous on $[a,b]\times[0,\frac\pi2]$, so we may differentiate under the integral sign:
$$f'(x)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{2x}{x^2+\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta.$$
Substitute $t=\tan\theta$, so $\sin^2\theta=\frac{t^2}{1+t^2}$ and $d\theta=\frac{dt}{1+t^2}$:
$$\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{x^2+\sin^2\theta}=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{x^2+(1+x^2)t^2}=\frac1{x\sqrt{1+x^2}}\arctan\!\Big(\frac{\sqrt{1+x^2}\,t}{x}\Big)\Big|_0^\infty=\frac{\pi}{2x\sqrt{1+x^2}}.$$
Hence
$$f'(x)=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x)=\pi\ln\!\big(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\big)+C.$$
Finding $C$ with the given integral. For $0\lt x\le1$ we have $\ln\sin^2\theta\le\ln(x^2+\sin^2\theta)\le\ln2$, and $\ln\sin\theta$ is integrable on $(0,\frac\pi2]$, so the limit $x\to0^+$ passes under the integral:
$$\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\ln\sin\theta\,d\theta=-\pi\ln2.$$
The formula gives $\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=\pi\ln1+C=C$, so $C=-\pi\ln2$.
Cross-check as $x\to\infty$. Write $\ln(x^2+\sin^2\theta)=2\ln x+\ln(1+\sin^2\theta/x^2)$ with $0\le\ln(1+\sin^2\theta/x^2)\le1/x^2$. Then $f(x)-\pi\ln x\to0$, while $\pi\ln(x+\sqrt{1+x^2})-\pi\ln x\to\pi\ln2$. This again gives $C=-\pi\ln 2$.
Answer:
$$f(x)=\pi\ln\!\Big(\frac{x+\sqrt{1+x^2}}{2}\Big)=\pi\big(\operatorname{arsinh}x-\ln2\big),\qquad x\gt0.$$
Numerically checked, e.g. $f(1)\approx0.5913$.
